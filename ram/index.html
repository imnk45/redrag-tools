How True RAM Latency Works (And Why It Matters for Gaming)

When buying RAM for a gaming PC, you are usually confronted with two prominent numbers on the box: the Data Rate (Speed) (e.g., 3200 MT/s, 3600 MT/s, 6000 MT/s) and the CAS Latency (CL) (e.g., CL16, CL30). But looking at just one of these numbers won't tell you how fast the RAM actually responds.

Our True RAM Latency Calculator solves this by converting these two confusing numbers into a single, universal metric: Nanoseconds (ns). By calculating the actual physical time it takes for the RAM to respond to a command from your CPU, you can easily compare any two RAM kits side-by-side.

DDR4 vs DDR5: The Latency Illusion

A common misconception when upgrading from a DDR4 system to a DDR5 system is that DDR5 is "slower" because the CAS Latency numbers are much higher. A standard DDR4 kit might be CL16, while a standard DDR5 kit is CL30.

However, latency is a factor of both CL and Speed. Because DDR5 operates at significantly higher frequencies, those clock cycles happen much faster. A DDR5-6000 CL30 kit has exactly the same true latency (10.00 ns) as a DDR4-3200 CL16 kit, but the DDR5 kit provides vastly more bandwidth, resulting in significantly better overall performance.

What is a "Good" Latency for Gaming?

In modern PC gaming (especially at 1080p and 1440p), lower RAM latency can significantly improve your 1% low framerates and reduce micro-stutters. Here is a general guide to what true latency numbers mean:

  • 12+ ns: Poor (Basic budget RAM, often causes bottlenecking in CPU-heavy games)
  • 10 - 11 ns: Average (Standard sweet-spot RAM, perfectly fine for most users)
  • 8.5 - 9.5 ns: Excellent (High-end enthusiast RAM, great for high-refresh-rate gaming)
  • Under 8.5 ns: Extreme (Overclocked B-Die or very tight DDR5, maximum performance)

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Is 3600MHz CL18 or 3200MHz CL16 faster?

If you put both into our calculator, you will see they both result in exactly 10.00 ns of true latency. They will feel identical in terms of responsiveness. However, the 3600MHz kit will provide slightly more total bandwidth, which can give it a tiny edge in specific scenarios like Ryzen Infinity Fabric scaling.

Why does my RAM say MHz instead of MT/s?

Historically, manufacturers marketed Double Data Rate (DDR) memory using "MHz" (Megahertz). However, this is technically incorrect. A DDR4-3200 kit actually runs at a clock frequency of 1600 MHz, but transfers data twice per clock cycle (Double Data Rate), resulting in 3200 MegaTransfers per second (MT/s). Today, MT/s is the correct and more accurate term, though MHz is still widely used in marketing.
